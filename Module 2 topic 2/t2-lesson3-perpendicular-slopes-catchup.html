Topic 1 Catch-Up · Lesson 6 · Activity 6.2 · Interpreting Graphs of Linear Functions · SE p. 243

Gracie's T-shirt sales

Gracie sells T-shirts for her mom at festivals and returns what she earns. The graph shows her potential earnings.

1. List as many facts as you can.

The line passes through (0, 0): no shirts, no money.

Up 15 for every 1 shirt: $15 per T-shirt. Selling 4 shirts earns $60.

Domain of the situation: whole numbers of shirts, 0 or more. Range: 0, 15, 30, 45, …

Activity 6.2, 20–25 minutes. To begin Day 2, have a student read the Essential Question aloud. Have pairs complete Questions 1–5, then share. Axes: x = Number of T-Shirts, y = Potential Earnings (dollars). The Ask Yourself: What do the slope, intercepts, domain, and range mean in this situation? Teacher-guide look-for: students give the domain and range of the function (all real numbers) rather than of the scenario (whole numbers of shirts, multiples of $15). Ask: Did you use one or two points to calculate the earnings per T-shirt?

Topic 1 Catch-Up · Lesson 6 · Activity 6.2 · Questions 2–5 · SE p. 244

Write and evaluate E(t)

2. Interpret the x- and y-intercepts.

Both are (0, 0): selling 0 shirts earns $0.

3. Write a function for her earnings.

E(t) = 15t

4. What does (t, E(t)) represent?

Each point on the line: t shirts sold and the dollars earned for them.

5. Evaluate and interpret.

E(2) = 30

(2, 30): 2 shirts earn $30.

E(5) = 75

(5, 75): 5 shirts earn $75.

E(2.75) = 41.25

Doesn't make sense: she can't sell 2.75 shirts.

Ask Yourself: Does E(t) represent a direct variation? (Yes: it passes through the origin and has the form y = kx.) Teacher-guide support: relate function notation back to x and y: (t, E(t)) is the same as (x, f(x)), which is the same as (x, y). Ask: Why doesn't the equation have a constant term? (She starts with $0.) E(2.75) is a point on the line, but it isn't a solution in the context.

Topic 1 Catch-Up · Lesson 6 · Activity 6.2 · Worked Example · SE p. 244

How many shirts to earn $100? Use the equation.

E(t) = 15t
100 = 15t
{{100|15}} = t
6.67 ≈ t

6a. What does t ≈ 6.67 mean?

6.67 shirts earns exactly $100. She can't sell part of a shirt: 6 shirts earn only $90, so she must sell 7 T-shirts.

6b. Why can you substitute 100 for E(t)?

E(t) is the dollars earned. $100 is the dollars she wants to earn.

Read the Worked Examples and complete Questions 6 and 7 as a class. The Worked Example is explicit instruction: have students restate each step in their own words. Ask: How do you know whether to substitute the given value for t or for E(t)? (It's dollars, so it's the output.) How did you decide 7 instead of 6? How much does she earn with 6 shirts? 7? ($90 and $105.)

Topic 1 Catch-Up · Lesson 6 · Activity 6.2 · Worked Example · SE p. 245

How many shirts to earn $100? Use the graph.

Graph each side of 100 = 15t as its own function: y = 100 and y = 15x. The solution is where they intersect.

Intersection: (6.67, 100). On the graph, 6.67 shirts; in context, 7 shirts.

7. Limitations of each method?

Equation: the answer may not make sense in context, so you must round up or down.

Graph: it can be hard to read an exact answer.

Remember box: the graph of an equation represents the set of all its solutions. Ask: Why does it make sense to rely on technology when using a graph? (Reading 6.67 by eye is hard.) How could you solve this using a table on a calculator? Stamp the learning, from the teacher guide: "An equation or a graph can be used to determine the value of an independent variable. Sometimes a graph can only give an approximate answer; however, using an equation always results in an exact answer." For more practice, assign Skills Practice Set B.

Day 1 · Getting Started · Coordinate Rotation · SE p. 306

Rotate AB, A(0, 0) and B(8, 5), about the origin

Original90°180°270°
A(0, 0)(0, 0)(0, 0)(0, 0)
B(8, 5)(−5, 8)(−8, −5)(5, −8)

Each 90° counterclockwise turn maps (x, y) to (−y, x). A is the center, so it stays.

Slopes: AB and A″B″: 5/8. A′B′ and A‴B‴: −8/5.

The two slopes are reciprocals with opposite signs: (5/8)(−8/5) = −1.

Getting Started, 15–20 minutes. Patty paper helps: trace the segment and the axes, then turn the paper a quarter turn.

Day 1 · Activity 3.1 · Slopes of Perpendicular Lines · SE p. 307 · ★ Essential

A line and its 90° rotation

Pair A

y = x
y = −x

Pair B

y = ⅓x + 5
y = −3x + 5

Pair C

y = −⅔x + 9
y = 3/2x − 4

Each line is rotated 90°, so the lines meet at a right angle: they are perpendicular.

The slopes of each pair are reciprocals with opposite signs.

Activity 3.1, 20–25 minutes. Students read the three graphs in their book (SE p. 307) and write the equations.

Day 1 · Activity 3.1 · Worked Example · SE p. 308 · ★ Essential

Proof: perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals

Given p ⊥ q. Point R(a, b) is on p.

Rotate R 90° counterclockwise about O: it lands on q at D(−b, a).

Slope of p: m₁ = b/a. Slope of q: m₂ = a/(−b).

−1/m₂ = −1 · (−b/a) = b/a = m₁

So m₁ = −1/m₂, and m₁ · m₂ = −1.

Have students restate each step. Question 3 asks for the same proof with a clockwise rotation: R(a, b) maps to D(b, −a), so m₂ = −a/b and again m₁ = −1/m₂.

Day 1 · Activity 3.1 · Question 4 · SE p. 309 · ★ Essential

Line j ⊥ line k. Find the slope of line k.

a.  m = ⅔
m = −3/2
b.  m = −⅘
m = 5/4
c.  m = −3
m = ⅓

Flip the fraction and change the sign. Check: the product is −1.

Stamp the learning: perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals. End of Day 1.

Warm up: work these on your own

1. Line j has slope ⅔. Line k is perpendicular to it. What is the slope of k? (SE p. 309)

0

Added for this deck; not from the textbook. Have this up as students walk in; they work silently while you take attendance (about 5 minutes). The same 4 questions are on the online Day 2 bell work. Reveal the answers one at a time. Answer key: 1. Perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals: ⅔ · (−3/2) = −1. 2. On a horizontal line y never changes, so the rise is 0. 3. Topic 1 Catch-Up · Lesson 6 · Activity 6.3 · Interpreting Changes to the Graph · SE p. 246

Gracie gives away 3 T-shirts in a raffle

Still $15 per shirt. The new relationship is G(t).

1a. How do the graphs show the price is the same?

The lines are parallel: same slope, 15.

1b. Interpret y = G(0).

(0, −45): with 0 shirts sold, earnings are −$45. That doesn't make sense in this situation.

1c. Interpret G(t) = 0.

(3, 0): after the 3 free shirts, she has $0 to return.

Activity 6.3, 20–25 minutes. Have pairs complete Questions 1–4, then share. The last two clicks label the points on the graph. Ask Yourself: Does G(t) vary directly with t? (No: it doesn't pass through the origin.) Ask: How do the parallel lines relate to the context? (Same price per shirt.) Does this notation represent the x-intercept or the y-intercept? (G(0) is the y-intercept; G(t) = 0 gives the x-intercept.) Look for the statements students use to interpret y = G(0) and G(t) = 0.

Topic 1 Catch-Up · Lesson 6 · Activity 6.3 · Questions 2–3 · SE p. 247

Two equations for G(t)

Brianna

Gracie gives away 3 T-shirts, so she has 3 fewer shirts to sell.

G(t) = 15(t − 3)

Hailey

The cost of giving away three shirts is $45.

G(t) = 15t − 45

2. Verify they are equivalent.

Distribute: 15(t − 3) = 15t − 45. ✓

3. How many shirts to earn $100?

100 = 15t − 45 → 145 = 15t → t ≈ 9.67
10 T-shirts
Ask: Which property did you apply to show the equations are equivalent? (The Distributive Property.) Explain how Brianna's and Hailey's perspectives are different: Brianna thinks in shirts (3 fewer to sell), Hailey thinks in dollars ($45 lost). Compare Question 3 with the Worked Example in Activity 6.2: on the graph, the line G(t) crosses y = 100 at about (9.67, 100), and in context she needs 10 shirts.

Day 2 · Activity 3.2 · Horizontal and Vertical Lines · SE pp. 310–311 · ★ Essential

Extend the segments into lines

Line p is vertical: x = 4. Lines q, r and s are horizontal: y = 4, y = 8, y = 12.

Horizontal

When x goes up 1, y doesn't change. Slope = 0/(x₂ − x₁) = 0.

Vertical

When y goes up 1, x doesn't change. Slope = (y₂ − y₁)/0: undefined.

Activity 3.2, 15–20 minutes. Memory trick from Topic 1: HOY VUX.

Day 2 · Talk the Talk · Things Aren't Always What They Seem · SE p. 314

Linh or Jackson?

Linh says lines a, b and c are parallel to each other and perpendicular to lines d, e and f. Jackson says they are parallel but not perpendicular to d, e, f.

Jackson is correct. The axes use different scales.

Lines a, b, c have slope 1, so they are parallel. Lines d, e, f have slope −¼.

1 · (−¼) ≠ −1: the slopes are not negative reciprocals, so the lines are not perpendicular.

Show the graph in the book (SE p. 314). The x-axis counts 1 per square and the y-axis 1 per 2 squares, so the lines look perpendicular but aren't.

Essential Question · Answered

How does rotation give perpendicular slopes?

A 90° rotation maps (x, y) to (−y, x), so a slope b/a becomes −a/b.

Perpendicular lines have negative reciprocal slopes: their product is −1.

Any vertical line is perpendicular to any horizontal line.

Have students answer the Essential Question on the lesson opener page.
