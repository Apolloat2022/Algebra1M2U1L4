Day 2 covers Activities 5.2 and 5.3. Day 3 covers Activity 5.4 and Talk the Talk. Materials: straightedges. Lesson 5 · SE p. 217

Today you will…

1. Construct linear equations to model relationships between two quantities.

2. Graph lines using slope-intercept form.

3. Graph lines using point-slope form.

4. Graph lines using standard form.

5. Convert equations from point-slope or standard form to slope-intercept form.

6. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of each form.

New key terms: standard form · x-intercept

Read each objective aloud as it appears. Ask: Which two forms do you already know? (slope-intercept and point-slope). Standard form is the new one. Have students write the two new key terms in their notes.

Essential Question · SE p. 217

You have graphed equations using tables of values.

Is there a more efficient method to graphing a linear relationship?

Can you use the equation of a linear relationship to create a graphical representation?

Have students write the Essential Question at the top of their notes. Don't answer it yet; we return to it at the end of each day. Quick check: ask a student how they would graph y = 2x + 1 right now. Most will say "make a table". That's the method this lesson replaces.

Day 1 · Getting Started · Jump In the Line · SE p. 218

What do you know from each equation?

Describe the graph. Then explain how you might graph the line from its equation.

1. y = {{2|3}}x + 7

Slope {{2|3}}, y-intercept (0, 7). Start at (0, 7), go up 2 and right 3 to (3, 9).

2. y − 3 = 5(x + 1)

Slope 5, point (−1, 3). Start at (−1, 3), go up 5 and right 1 to (0, 8).

3. x = −4

A vertical line through (−4, 0). The slope is undefined.

4. −3x + 8y = 10

The slope and y-intercept aren't easy to see. Make a table: (2, 2) and (10, 5) are on the line.

Getting Started, 10–15 minutes. Have pairs work Questions 1–4 before you reveal anything, then share as a class. For #2, how did you get the signs of the point? (x + 1 means x₁ = −1.) For #4, ask: How could you work with this equation to find its slope or points on it? That question sets up Activity 5.3.

Day 1 · Activity 5.1 · Using Slope-Intercept Form to Graph a Line · SE p. 219

Douglas's concert tickets

Douglas has 10 tickets left. He gives a pair of tickets to each person who asks. Let x = people who ask and y = tickets left.

y = −2x + 10

Plot the y-intercept (0, 10) first. Then use the slope to count to more points.

Slope is rise over run. You can write m = −2 as two different ratios:

{{−2|1}}

negative rise, positive run: down 2, right 1

{{2|−1}}

positive rise, negative run: up 2, left 1

Activity 5.1, 25–30 minutes in total. Ask a student to read the introduction aloud. Ask: Why is the slope negative? (Tickets go down as more people ask.) Why −2? (A pair per person.) The sign of the number tells you which way to go. Both ratios equal −2; using both lets you plot points on each side of the starting point. Optional, from the teacher guide: before the Worked Example, let students try graphing the context on their own, then connect their graph to the Worked Example.

Day 1 · Activity 5.1 · Worked Example · SE p. 219

Graph y = −2x + 10

1. Plot the y-intercept, (0, 10).

2. For m = {{−2|1}}, go down 2 and right 1: (1, 8).

3. For m = {{2|−1}}, go up 2 and left 1: (−1, 12).

4. Connect the points to form a straight line.

A rule of thumb when graphing a line is to plot at least three points.

This Worked Example is explicit instruction: go through it as a class and have students restate each step in their own words. Each click plots one point, in the order of steps 1–3, and the fourth click draws the line. Teacher-guide idea: have students highlight (0, 10) and the two slope ratios in different colors, and mark the matching moves on the graph in the same colors. The line crosses the x-axis at (5, 0): after 5 people ask, Douglas is out of tickets.

2. y = −{{5|2}}x + 3

Day 1 · Activity 5.1 · Question 3 · SE p. 221

Watch the scale: y = 10x + 25

The y-axis counts by 10, not by 1.

Start at (0, 25), halfway between 20 and 30.

Write the slope as {{10|1}}: up 10 units, right 1 unit.

Count units, not grid squares. Up 10 is only one square here.

Use a straightedge to draw your lines.

The teacher guide flags this one: students count grid spaces instead of units. On this grid one square up is 10 units, so a rise of 10 is one square. Each click plots one point; the fourth draws the line. Ask: Why does it help to write the number 10 as a fraction, 10/1?

Day 1 · Activity 5.1 · Question 4 · SE p. 221

Avery's headset savings

Avery has $30 saved and plans to mow lawns for $15 each. Write an equation, then graph it.

Let x = lawns mowed and y = total savings in dollars.

Starts at $30 → (0, 30). Earns $15 per lawn → slope 15.

y = 15x + 30

Graph: start at (0, 30), then up 15 and right 1 for each lawn.

Should the graph have arrows or points on each end? Avery can't mow −1 lawns, so the graph starts at (0, 30) with a closed point.

Remind students to label the axes with the quantities: Number of Lawns Mowed and Total Savings (dollars). The teacher guide's equation is y = 30 + 15x, which is the same thing. Ask: Why is this graph increasing? Use the graph to show how total savings goes up by 15 for every lawn. Discuss the Ask Yourself: a closed circle at (0, 30) because 0 lawns is possible; an arrow to the right because he can keep mowing. Strictly, only whole numbers of lawns make sense; accept either a ray or points, as long as the reasoning is sound. Each click plots one point, then the line.

Day 1 · Activity 5.1 · Question 5 · SE p. 222

The scuba diver

A scuba diver is 130 feet below sea level. The diver ascends at a constant rate of 20 feet per minute.

Let x = time in minutes and y = depth in feet.

Below sea level → (0, −130). Rising → slope +20.

y = 20x − 130

Graph: start at (0, −130), then up 20 and right 1 each minute.

The diver reaches the surface (y = 0) after 6.5 minutes.

Common error: a slope of −20 because the depth numbers are negative. Ask: Is the diver going up or down? So is the depth increasing or decreasing? The teacher guide writes it as y = −130 + 20x. The graph lives below the x-axis because depth below sea level is negative. Each click plots one point, then the line. Questions 1–3 are a good check of today's content; for more practice graphing from slope-intercept form, assign Skills Practice Set A.

Day 1 · Activity 5.1 · Summary

Graphing from slope-intercept form

1. Plot the y-intercept (0, b).

2. Write the slope m as a ratio, {{rise|run}}. Count from the y-intercept to plot another point.

3. Use the equivalent ratio (both signs flipped) to plot a point on the other side.

4. Connect at least three points with a straightedge. Check the direction: positive slope rises left to right, negative slope falls.

Stamp the learning, from the teacher guide: "An equation written in slope-intercept form can be graphed by first plotting the y-intercept and then using the slope to locate two additional points by counting from that initial point." To close Day 1, have students reread the Essential Question and read the activity summary to the class. End of Day 1.

Day 2 · Activity 5.2 · Using Point-Slope Form to Graph a Line · SE p. 223

The jazz band's account

They already had some money. They sell tickets at $5.00 each. After selling 3 tickets, they had $50. Let x = tickets sold, y = money in the account.

a. $5 per ticket → slope 5. 3 tickets, $50 → point (3, 50).

y − 50 = 5(x − 3)

b. Plot the known point, (3, 50).

Write the slope as {{5|1}} (up 5, right 1) and as {{−5|−1}} (down 5, left 1). Count from the point.

Plot at least two more points, then connect them.

Activity 5.2, 15–20 minutes. Have a student read the introduction aloud. Ask: Why does point-slope form make sense here? (We know a point and a rate, not the starting amount.) The text steps come first; then each click plots a point, (3, 50), (4, 55), (2, 45), and the last click draws the line. Other points include (5, 60) and (6, 65). Ask: How is this the same as and different from graphing from slope-intercept form? (Same counting; you start from the given point instead of the y-intercept.) The line hits the y-axis at 35: the band started with $35.

Identify the slope and a point, then graph

a. y − 5 = −{{3|4}}(x − 14)

m = −{{3|4}}, point (14, 5). Up 3, left 4.

b. y + 8 = {{1|2}}(x + 6)

Day 2 · Activity 5.2 · Question 3 · SE p. 225

The underwater ROV

An ROV is lowered from a boat at 0.3 meters per second. After 90 seconds, it is at a depth of −25.8 meters.

Let x = time in seconds and y = depth in meters.

Going down → slope −0.3. After 90 s at −25.8 m → point (90, −25.8).

y − (−25.8) = −0.3(x − 90)
y + 25.8 = −0.3(x − 90)

To graph, write the slope as {{−6|20}}: down 6 m for every 20 s.

Look for different scales on the x-axis (some students may use minutes); our grid counts seconds by 20. Ask: How did you rewrite the rate to use it as the slope? (Lowered means negative: −0.3, which is −3/10 or −6/20.) Each click plots one point, then the line. Extension: in slope-intercept form, y = −0.3x + 1.2, so the ROV started 1.2 m above the water (on the boat) and reached the surface after 4 seconds. Note: the teacher guide's sample answer says x is in minutes, but the problem gives the rate per second, so use seconds.

Day 2 · Activity 5.3 · Standard Form of a Linear Equation · SE p. 226

Tickets for the school play

Student tickets cost $5, adult tickets cost $8. Opening night collected $1600. Let x = student tickets and y = adult tickets.

5x + 8y = 1600

Key term: standard form

Ax + By = C

A, B, and C are integers, and A and B are not both zero.

1a. 5x: money from student tickets ($5 times the number sold)

1b. 8y: money from adult tickets ($8 times the number sold)

1c. 1600: the total money collected

2. Either variable could be independent. The number of student tickets could depend on the number of adult tickets, or the other way around.

Activity 5.3, 20–25 minutes. Ask a student to read the introduction and definition aloud. Have pairs work Questions 1–5, then share. Ask: Why can't A and B both be 0? (Then there would be no x or y at all.) Why is standard form a good fit here? (The total comes from adding two kinds of tickets.) Why is it hard to tell which quantity is independent?

Day 2 · Activity 5.3 · Questions 3–5 · SE p. 227

Graph using the intercepts

Key term: the x-intercept (x, 0) is where a line crosses the x-axis. To find it, substitute 0 for y.

3. y = 0: 5x = 1600, so x = 320 → (320, 0). 320 student tickets and no adult tickets.

x = 0: 8y = 1600, so y = 200 → (0, 200). 200 adult tickets and no student tickets.

5. Slope = {{200 − 0|0 − 320}} = −{{5|8}}. For every 8 more student tickets, 5 fewer adult tickets.

Remind students: the y-intercept (0, b) is where x is 0, and the x-intercept (x, 0) is where y is 0. After the text steps, each click plots an intercept and the last click connects them (Question 4). Ask: Use the graph to name another pair that makes sense, such as (160, 100). Slope check: 8 student tickets bring in $40, the same as 5 adult tickets. Look for sign errors when students divide the y-intercept by the x-intercept instead of using the slope formula.

Day 2 · Activity 5.3 · Worked Example · SE p. 230

Standard form from two points: (−1, 3) and (−4, −3)

First, find the slope.

m = {{−3 − 3|−4 − (−1)}} = {{−6|−3}} = 2

Next, pick a point and write point-slope form.

y − 3 = 2(x − (−1)) → y − 3 = 2(x + 1)

Then, rewrite in standard form.

y − 3 = 2x + 2 → y = 2x + 5
−2x + y = 5
Analyze the Worked Example together as a class. The last step subtracts 2x from both sides so x and y are on the same side. Ask: Why do we need the slope first? Which form would you prefer when you are given two points? 2x − y = −5 is also correct; it's the same equation multiplied by −1.

Day 3 · Talk the Talk · Choose Your Medium · SE p. 232

Joey's math quiz

2 points for each correct multiple-choice question, 3 points for each correct short answer. Joey earned 36 points. Let x = multiple-choice correct and y = short answer correct.

2x + 3y = 36

Form: standard form, because the equation comes straight from the context: two kinds of points add up to one total.

Graph: use the intercepts. y = 0 gives (18, 0); x = 0 gives (0, 12).

Day 3 · Talk the Talk · Question 2 · SE p. 233

Nicky's savings

Nicky has $20 and plans to save an additional $10 every two weeks. Let x = number of weeks and y = total savings.

$10 every 2 weeks is $5 per week. He starts with $20.

y = 5x + 20

Form: slope-intercept, because the rate and the starting amount are given directly.

Graph: plot (0, 20), then use the slope, {{10|2}}: up 10, right 2.

Ask: Does y = 5x + 20 or y = 5x − 20 represent this? Is it in slope-intercept or point-slope form? The trap is a slope of 10: the $10 is every two weeks, so the rate is $5 per week. Writing the slope as 10/2 makes the graph easy: up 10, right 2. Also accept point-slope form, such as y − 30 = 5(x − 2).

Advantages and disadvantages of each form

FormAdvantagesDisadvantages
Slope-intercept
y = mx + b		Shows the slope and y-intercept. Easy to write from a starting value and a rate. Graph from the intercept and slope.Less helpful if the y-intercept doesn't make sense or is off the part of the graph you need.
Point-slope
y − y1 = m(x − x1)		Shows the slope and a point. Easy to write from two points or a point and a rate. Graph from the point and slope.Doesn't show the y-intercept.
Standard
Ax + By = C		Fits contexts where two amounts add to a total. Both intercepts are easy to find and graph.You have to calculate the slope.
