Welcome. Today's topic is another way to write the equation of a line. Point out the color code: orange always means slope, blue always means a point. The lesson runs two days. Day 1 covers Getting Started and Activities 4.1–4.2. Day 2 covers Activity 4.3 and Talk the Talk.

Lesson 4 · SE p. 201

Today you will…

1. Use the slope formula to derive the point-slope form of a linear equation.

2. Write an equation in point-slope form to model a linear relationship between two quantities.

3. Write equations for vertical and horizontal lines.

Read each objective aloud as it appears. Ask: Which of these words do you already know? (slope formula, slope-intercept form). Tell students that 'derive' means we will build the new form ourselves from something we already know.

Essential Question · SE p. 201

You already know slope-intercept form, y = mx + b.

Are there other forms of a linear equation that you can use?

How do you write equations for horizontal and vertical lines?

Have students write the Essential Question at the top of their notes. Don't answer it yet; we return to it at the end of each day. Quick check: ask a student what m and b mean in y = mx + b.

Day 1 · Getting Started · Draining the Pool · SE p. 202

Pool A

Miguel and Nia drain the community pools. Write each equation in slope-intercept form, y = mx + b.

Pool A is at a water level of 14 feet and drains at a rate of 3 feet per hour.

Let x = hours of draining and y = water level in feet.

It starts at 14 feet, so the y-intercept is b = 14.

The level goes down 3 feet each hour, so the slope is negative: m = −3.

y = −3x + 14
Getting Started, 10–15 minutes. Have pairs try Pool A before you reveal anything. Common error: writing +3 instead of −3. Ask: Is the water level going up or down? So is the slope positive or negative? Define the variables before writing the equation. The graph appears last (x = hours, y = feet of water): the line starts at 14 on the y-axis and drops 3 feet for every hour. Ask: When is the pool empty? (After 4⅔ hours.)

Day 1 · Getting Started · Draining the Pool · SE p. 202

Pool B

Pool B is at a water level of 10 feet after draining for 3 hours and drains at a rate of 2 feet per hour.

We know a point: (3, 10)

We know the slope: m = −2

We don't know b. Substitute into y = mx + b:

10 = −2(3) + b
10 = −6 + b
b = 16
y = −2x + 16
Ask: What's different from Pool A? (We don't know the starting level.) Stress that '10 feet after 3 hours' is the point (3, 10). Reveal the substitution one line at a time; have students do each step on whiteboards before you click. The graph appears last: the point we were given, (3, 10), is on the line, and following it back to the y-axis gives the starting level b = 16. The pool is empty after 8 hours.

Day 1 · Getting Started · Draining the Pool · SE p. 202

Pool C

Pool C is at 15 feet after 2 hours and at 12 feet after 4 hours.

Two points: (2, 15) and (4, 12)

Step 1: find the slope.

m = {{12 − 15|4 − 2}} = −{{3|2}}

Step 2: find b using (2, 15).

15 = −{{3|2}}(2) + b
15 = −3 + b
b = 18
y = −{{3|2}}x + 18
Now we know neither the slope nor the intercept, only two points. Ask students to recall the slope formula before you reveal it. Check the answer together: at x = 2, −3/2(2) + 18 = 15. It works. The graph appears last (x = hours, y = feet of water): point out that the line starts at 18 feet on the y-axis, which is b, and that the pool is empty after 12 hours.

Connect each answer to b, the slope, and where the line crosses the x-axis.

Write an equation from a table

First, calculate the slope.

m = {{y2 − y1|x2 − x1}} = {{5 − 6|4 − 2}} = −{{1|2}}

Next, choose any point from the table: (2, 6)

Then, substitute the slope, (2, 6), and an unknown point (x, y) into the slope formula.

Finally, multiply both sides by (x − 2) so no variable is in a denominator.

Activity 4.1, 20–25 minutes in total. This Worked Example is explicit instruction: go through each step as a class and have students restate it in their own words. Key idea: (x, y) stands for ANY point on the line, which is why it stays as variables.

Key Term · SE p. 203

Point-slope form

y − y1 = m(x − x1)

m is the slope of the line.

(x1, y1) is any point on the line. It does not have to be the y-intercept.

Example: slope 3 and point (1, 4)

y − 4 = 3(x − 1)
Have students copy the definition into their notes, using the colors. Stress that (x₁, y₁) can be any point, not only the y-intercept. In the example, ask a student to name m, x₁, and y₁ before you reveal the equation.

Day 1 · Activity 4.1 · SE p. 203

Where does point-slope form come from?

Start with the slope formula. Use a known point (x1, y1) and any point (x, y) on the line.

m = {{y − y1|x − x1}}

Multiply both sides by (x − x1).

m(x − x1) = y − y1

Swap the sides.

y − y1 = m(x − x1)
This meets Objective 1. Point-slope form is not a new formula to memorize; it is the slope formula with the denominator multiplied away. If students forget point-slope form, they can always rebuild it from the slope formula.

Day 1 · Activity 4.1 · Question 1 · SE p. 203

Rewrite it in slope-intercept form

Solve the Worked Example equation for y.

y − 6 = −{{1|2}}(x − 2)

Distribute −{{1|2}}:

y − 6 = −{{1|2}}x + 1

Add 6 to both sides:

y = −{{1|2}}x + 7

Point-slope form shows

the slope and a point, which may not be the y-intercept.

Slope-intercept form shows

the slope and the y-intercept.

Same: both show the slope, and both describe the same line.

Complete Question 1 as a class. Watch the distribution step: −1/2 times −2 is +1, a common sign error. Discussion: both forms describe the same line, so neither is more correct; each one makes different information easy to see.

Day 1 · Activity 4.1 · Question 6 · SE p. 204

A line through (35, 102) and (42, 20)

On the graph in your book, the y-intercept is not visible. Point-slope form doesn't need it.

Step 1: calculate the slope.

m = {{20 − 102|42 − 35}} = −{{82|7}}

Step 2: choose either point. Using (42, 20):

y − 20 = −{{82|7}}(x − 42)

Using (35, 102) also works: y − 102 = −{{82|7}}(x − 35). Both describe the same line.

The graph in the student book doesn't show where the line crosses the y-axis, which is exactly when point-slope form is most useful. Either point gives a correct equation. If time allows, have half the class use each point and compare by converting both to slope-intercept form. For extra practice, assign Skills Practice Set B.

Essential Question · Answered

What did we learn?

Yes, there's another form. Point-slope form uses the slope and any point on the line:

y − y1 = m(x − x1)

Horizontal lines have slope 0 and equations like y = 11.

Vertical lines have an undefined slope and equations like x = −2.

Students answer the Essential Question on the lesson opener page. Ask them to mention both forms and both special lines in their answer.
